Interaction design · Working prototypes

Can a screen teach a skill without explaining it?

Outside class I build drills: small apps where something is learned through the hands rather than through text. After twenty of them, the useful part isn't any single app — it's the rules they forced me to write down.

Most learning software explains, then quizzes. My drills try to skip the explanation: a Japanese abacus whose beads guide mental arithmetic and then disappear; sentence structure assembled by fitting pieces together instead of drawing a tree; a keyboard map where each symbol sits where the movement goes, so the screen performs the motion instead of describing it.

The rule that came from FormDrill

FormDrill trains you to see the logical form of an argument apart from its subject. Building it, every time a round failed, my instinct was to add something — a hint, a scenario, an example. Each addition made it worse. The fix was always structural.

When the design is wrong, change the structure of the exercise. Don't add a hint or a scenario around it.

Twenty principles

That correction became the first of twenty named principles, each stating a common mistake and its fix. A few of them:

  • Removable Scaffold — a support that never comes off has failed; design the support and its removal together.
  • Direct Action — make the user's motion the state change, not a signal that triggers one.
  • Winning Condition — write one precise sentence for “you've got it” before anything else; it decides what to show and what would train the wrong thing.
  • Progressive Unlock — start with the smallest move set that is still a real activity, and add one capability at a time.

They now decide how each new piece is designed, and they explain old decisions. In Tetris Rotation, each of the three rotations has its own colour and body — and those colours are content, not theme: changing them would change what the screen teaches.

Knowing what to cut

Unparse once had roguelike systems on top of its core. They were built, played, and removed in July 2026, along with a rule to keep one core system and no sentence with two possible analyses. The drill got smaller and better.
