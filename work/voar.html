Why does an NGO lose the donors who aren't companies?

Associação Voar keeps whole programmes alive on a single corporate donor, while the people who give a little every month barely hear from it. We went to find out why, designed an app around the gap, tested it, and wrote it up.

Design Research, Unifor · semester 2025.2 · group project

CourseDesign Research
MethodDouble Diamond
OutputApp prototype · paper
PublishedISSN 2965-9396

Associação Voar works with children, teenagers and their families in vulnerable situations in Fortaleza, offering education, culture, sport and leisure. On our first visit to its headquarters, the staff mentioned, almost in passing, that entire projects depended on a single company's contribution.

That sentence set the question for the semester. If one corporate donor carries a project, what happens to the individual donor — the person who gives twenty reais a month? Nationally the picture was already worrying: the share of Brazilians who donated money fell from 66% in 2015 to 52% in 2022, and trust in NGOs fell with it.

The problem

Voar depends on a few big donors, and it doesn’t keep the small, recurring ones.

Each of us wrote what we had seen and heard on the visit as short notes on a shared board. The same room is used for meals, prayer and jiu-jitsu. There is no HR. The library is small and disorganised. There is a waiting list. The association follows 150 children in Fortaleza and 300 more in Tauá. We sorted the notes into needs, pains and opportunities.

Finding out before designing

We followed the Double Diamond and kept the first half honest: no solution until the problem was understood. The problem statement was short: Voar raises too little through donations, and the lack of money affects its work with children and teenagers. We wrote three hypotheses. The association has no effective way of telling people about its impact. It has no ongoing relationship with current or potential donors. And it depends on one-off gifts, with no plan for recurring ones.

The field notes and a round of desk research on giving went into a CSD matrix — what we were certain of, what we supposed, what we doubted. Some of the certainties came from the reading: people give more when they see others giving, and the story of one identifiable person raises more than statistics about many. Among the doubts were the ones that mattered most later: where does the money actually come from, are the current donation channels easy, and what is the first message a potential donor sees?

The doubts became a semi-structured interview script, and we ran three interviews with Voar staff to close them: the coordinator, the person in charge of finance, and the executive director. We transcribed them and cut the answers into short quotes.

What the interviews said

Most of the money comes from companies and public funding calls. Individual donors are a smaller and less predictable front: “they don’t follow a pattern — sometimes they give, sometimes they don’t, sometimes a bit more, sometimes a bit less.” About 70% of the budget goes to paying people, and the association can see roughly three months ahead before the projections turn negative.

Most individuals give by Pix. Some give through a third-party donation platform, which keeps part of every gift, asks for a long sign-up, and drops the donor automatically when their card expires. Staff had started asking new donors to send a Pix directly instead, to avoid the fee. A sponsorship campaign had once used the platform for recurring gifts, but it wasn’t kept up and the donors drifted away.

Many of the individual donors come from the church where Voar began, and they give because they know the place and the people. Apart from the executive director’s visits to the larger donors, there was no ongoing relationship with them at all.

“People don’t give to a number. They give to a person.”

That line, from one of the interviews, stayed with us. The problem wasn’t that people didn’t want to give. It was that giving was a one-way door.

From pains to a product

In an affinity session we grouped the quotes until patterns appeared, and set a goal for the product: keep the donors Voar already has, bring them closer, and show what each gift actually did. Three requirements survived. A simpler way in, so a first donation doesn’t start with a long sign-up. A channel Voar controls — we looked at recurring Pix — so no fraction of each gift is lost and an expired card doesn’t end the relationship. And continuous communication: stories of the children, and updates on where the money goes.

We aimed it at the smaller, recurring donors, the group the association had no staff to look after by hand. We benchmarked ShareTheMeal, the UN World Food Programme’s app, as the reference for a low-friction donation, looked again at the platform Voar used, whose form we marked as a long sign-up, and drew four task flows: sign up and donate; follow the association’s activities and volunteer openings; buy from a small shop; and the donor’s own profile.

Testing it

We tested the low-fidelity prototype by having people use the application and carry out tasks in it. What we saw went into a mid-fidelity digital prototype — the version we delivered. The first digital version on our board has four tabs that follow the flows: Donate, once or monthly, ending in a Pix code; Community, with stories and volunteer openings; Shop, with campaigns and products; and Profile, with the monthly gift and a history of donations.

Notes left beside the screens on the board show what was still open. Let people type any amount, not only the preset ones. Add a place for feedback on donations. Let the history be viewed by date. And don’t put the total someone has given at the top of their profile — seeing how much you have spent isn’t always pleasant.

From project to paper

The project became an experience report, published in the proceedings of the VI Encontro de Curricularização da Extensão at Unifor in 2026. An expanded version is being adapted for IHC 2026, the Brazilian Symposium on Human Factors in Computing Systems.
