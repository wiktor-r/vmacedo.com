Editorial design

Can a magazine about breaking norms run on a strict grid?

RUPTURA is a magazine about decolonial music — “self-expression that goes beyond the norm of colonial music.” The pages are about rupture; the system underneath them is anything but.

InDesign

A magazine has to be read before it can be felt. For RUPTURA we wanted lines of music to run loose across the pages — trails of notes looping over text and images — and the only way that stays readable is if everything else holds still.

The grid first

We built the grid by our professor's method. The narrowest column is the width of the lowercase alphabet set in the body face, Circe at 10 pt; the widest is one and a half times that. The gutter equals the leading. The baseline grid starts at zero and steps by the leading, 13 pt, and the margins are the leading too. Sizes scale with their leading, so 10/13 becomes 20/26: every line in the magazine lands on the same rhythm.

Four acts

The 44 pages are organised like a record: four acts of eight pages, with a double-page ad as the interval. Every story is one of six types — special report, interview, profile, essay, visual essay or column — and every act contains a visual essay, so the reader never goes eight pages without a spread that is mostly image.

The fixed pages sit around them: cover, editorial, masthead, contents, letters, five ads. One decision the group took deliberately was to keep the back cover free of advertising and give it to a column instead.

The music is allowed to wander because the text never does.

One file for the page plan

The page plan lived in one data file: the items in order, each with its page count, so the numbering, the contents page and the printed flatplan all came from the same source. Moving a story moved everything after it, correctly.
