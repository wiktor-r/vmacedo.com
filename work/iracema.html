Wayfinding · Field research

What does a beach need to tell the people walking on it?

The Praia de Iracema waterfront mixes landmarks, public facilities and years of old signs. Before drawing a single sign, our team of five needed to know what was there and where — so I built us a map to find out.

Signage starts with an inventory. Which signs exist, which should go, where people arrive, where they hesitate. On a busy waterfront that inventory is big, and with five people collecting it, the hard part is keeping one shared picture instead of five partial ones.

So before the signs, I built a tool: an interactive 3D map of the waterfront — every building extruded from OpenStreetMap over satellite imagery — that the whole team could open by link and drop pins on.

One hundred pins

The field survey ended with a hundred points on the map: public facilities, points of interest, existing signs, signs to remove, and the pieces we were proposing. That map became the location map we delivered, and every proposed sign got its justification from its pin — why here, for whom, pointing where.

The survey also changed the system. It had marked five "directional plates", but our family had only one directional piece, so all five became directional totems. Two pins fifty-four metres apart turned out to be the same decision, and became one totem carrying the destinations the team had written on both.

A language from the shore

Three icons from the waterfront itself — the sail, the sea and the sunset — gave the system its language: five colours named after the place (sand, sunset, light sea, mid sea, deep sea), Avenir Next, and text at a 7.1 : 1 contrast. The family runs from an urban totem you can see from far away, through the directional totem and the interpretive sign, down to a small indicative sign.

The sign that came down

Feedback arrived in rounds, and one piece shows how decisions moved. The indicative sign for the bike rack started at 180 cm. It came down to 160 to keep its text inside the comfortable viewing band. Then we lowered it again, to 120 cm, below that band on purpose: it is an arrival piece, read by someone standing next to their bike, not across a promenade. The board says so, so nobody mistakes the decision for an error.

Every number on the boards came from one table, so a feedback round cost minutes, not days.

Built to the standard, generated from data

The boards give front, side and top views with every dimension, materials chosen for salt air and sun, and accessibility following NBR 9050: a braille strip at 90–95 cm, tactile relief, QR codes with audio and a matte finish against glare.

Behind them is a single spreadsheet — one row per sign, with its type, survey pin, coordinates and justification. The boards, the presentation and the parts list were all generated from it. When the class asked for bigger text, darker greys, centimetres instead of millimetres, or one sign moved, we changed the table and rebuilt everything, and the documents never disagreed with each other.
