What does a beach need to tell the people walking on it?

The Praia de Iracema waterfront mixes landmarks, public facilities and years of old signs. Before drawing a single sign, our team of five needed one picture of the shore: what stands where, which signs are already there, and where a visitor has to choose. So I built a 3D map to hold it.

Signage work starts with an inventory: every sign already on the waterfront, every place a visitor might look for, every corner where someone has to pick a direction. On a busy waterfront that inventory is big, and with five people collecting it, the hard part is keeping one shared picture instead of five partial ones.

The team began marking it on a shared FigJam board, over a site plan of the area. But the team also needed to plan the signs in context, deciding where each piece goes on the waterfront, and the software available to us couldn’t handle that.

So I built the 3D map. It took the survey marks off the flat plan and stood them on the ground, between the real buildings, at real coordinates. It is a single web page: anyone on the team could open it by link, turn the model around, and add or move a point.

What the map is made of

I extruded every building from its OpenStreetMap outline. Where the record has a measured height, the block uses it. Where it only has a number of floors, the height is floors × 3.2 m. The 41 buildings with neither stand at a default 6 m. I downloaded the data on 2 October 2026.

Under the buildings I laid Esri’s satellite imagery, about 0.3 m per pixel around the neighbourhood and coarser further out, stored inside the page. In the default view the team’s site plan is laid over the ground as white lines, with the sea cut out of it so the real water shows through. The neighbourhood boundary is dashed, landmarks are labelled, and in the satellite view cultural buildings such as the Estoril are picked out in red.

The page also says what it is not. Outlines and heights are collaborative records, not a measured survey, so anything that depends on a dimension has to be checked on site.

Putting the survey on the ground

Each finding became a pin, a line or an area, in the same categories as the team’s board. They fall into two groups. One describes the place and the plan: public facilities, points of interest, and the signs the team wanted to add, by type. The other is the field survey itself: existing signs, signs to remove, improvised signs, places where accessibility is missing, pedestrian flow and event areas.

By 20 October the plan held 100 items: 69 pins, 14 lines and 17 areas. Eighty-nine of them still carry the id of the FigJam note they started as; the other eleven were added on the map itself. Every item keeps its coordinates and whatever the team wrote on it.

Seen from the sea, the stretch between the Ponte dos Ingleses and the Estoril shows what the inventory looked like. Four existing signs stand within about forty metres of each other at the entrance to the pier. Out at the end of it, a sign marked for removal sits seven metres from the spot where the team wanted an interpretive one. Along the promenade, improvised “don’t litter” signs stand among the official ones.

From pins to pieces

On 20 October the 100 items were frozen and copied into the delivery folder, so later edits to the live map could not move anything on the boards. The parts spreadsheet gave each piece a row, with the pin it came from.

The pins settled several decisions. The survey had five “directional plates”, and our family had only one directional piece, so all five became directional totems. One of those plates stood 54 metres from a pin for a directional totem, and the team had written four destinations into it: right to Inst. Iracema and the Estoril, straight on to the Ponte dos Ingleses, left to the São Rafael school. The pair became one totem carrying those destinations, number 2 on the delivered map, which the team later moved to the promenade just west of the pier.

Other pieces took the place of what the survey said should go. Totem 3 replaces an improvised sign on a post at the Rua dos Cariris crossing. Totem 5 stands where a festival sign is to be removed. The interpretive sign at the end of the Ponte dos Ingleses replaces the old one there. The Estoril got an interpretive sign because the survey found none on the historic building, and the bike rack by the Pavilhão Atlântico got an indicative sign because it had nothing at all.

Back to a flat map

The final positions were set in 2D. The same data — the OpenStreetMap outlines, the satellite tiles stored in the page, and a clean vector redraw of the site plan — was flattened into a map for the FigJam board, and on 22 October the team placed the 37 pieces there, over the survey pins. That map became the location map the team delivered.

Because the flat map and the model share one coordinate system, a position on the board converts straight back to metres on the model, at 8 pixels per metre. A piece within a metre of a survey pin keeps that pin as its source: 16 of the 37 do. The other 21 were placed on the board. The signs placed on survey pins carried their justification from them: why here, for whom, pointing where.

Some things the map could not settle. Which way totem 2 faces, and so where its arrows point, stayed on the checklist to confirm on site.

A language from the shore

Three icons from the waterfront itself — the sail, the sea and the sunset — gave the system its language: five colours named after the place (sand, sunset, light sea, mid sea, deep sea), Avenir Next, and text at a 7.1 : 1 contrast.

The family runs from an urban totem you can see from far away, through the directional totem and the interpretive sign, down to a small indicative sign.

The sign that came down

Feedback arrived in rounds, and one piece shows how decisions moved. The indicative sign for the bike rack started at 180 cm. It came down to 160 to keep its text inside the comfortable viewing band.

Then the team lowered it again, to 120 cm, below that band on purpose: it is an arrival piece, read by someone standing next to their bike, not across a promenade. The board says so, so nobody mistakes the decision for an error.

Every number on the boards came from one table, so a feedback round cost minutes, not days.

Built to the standard, generated from data

The boards give front, side and top views with every dimension, materials chosen for salt air and sun, and accessibility following NBR 9050: a braille strip at 90–95 cm, tactile relief, QR codes with audio and a matte finish against glare.

Behind them is that single spreadsheet — one row per sign, with its type, survey pin, coordinates and justification. The boards, the presentation and the parts list were all generated from it.

When the class asked for bigger text, darker greys, centimetres instead of millimetres, or one sign moved, the team changed the table and rebuilt everything, and the documents never disagreed with each other.
