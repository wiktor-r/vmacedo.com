Sappin · UX research · Information architecture

What do small illustrators in Ceará actually need?

Sappin is an app for local illustrators in Ceará. We set out to help them sell their work and reach people outside the state. Four interviews later the problem looked different: what they missed most was guidance on the professional side of art.

User Experience (G716), Unifor · semester 2025.2 · group project

ProjectSappin, an app for local illustrators
CourseUser Experience (G716)
MethodGarrett’s five planes
OutputRequirements · flows · site map · sketches

The problem came first. Local illustrators in Ceará sell mostly in the regional market, find it hard to reach anyone outside the state, and see their work used without permission or payment. That cuts their income and keeps people in Ceará away from their own artists.

The brief we wrote for ourselves asked for a digital product that brings the community and local illustrators together, to help with promotion, sales and the region’s cultural identity. A note on the board warned against rushing: don’t think about the product yet, we aren’t there.

We worked through Jesse James Garrett’s five planes of user experience, in order: strategy, scope, structure, skeleton, surface. The board follows that line literally. It runs left to right, one red title bar per plane, joined by a single arrow from “Início” to “Definitivo”.

I took part in every stage except the visual identity, which others on the team designed.

Reading before asking

The research plan listed what we wanted to know, where to look and which words to search for: papers, reports from UNESCO, the Ministry of Culture, IPEA, Sebrae and IBGE, art sales platforms, and local cultural policy. The desk research was split into one block per team member, and feedback notes on the board pushed it further: one block was too short, another should explain its source in our own words, and every number we found should be kept for defending the theme later.

The reading went into a CSD matrix: what we were certain of, what we supposed, and what we doubted. We were certain the internet helps link artists with buyers, and that events connect artists with each other, not only with customers. We supposed the average person in Ceará isn’t familiar with local visual artists, and that most artists have a second job. The doubts were the questions only people could answer: what share of local artists sell online, and what is the main income of those who can’t live on art?

Alongside the matrix we drafted four proto-personas: an artist already part of the local scene, an events organiser, a family man as an average consumer, and a psychology student who would rather be drawing. They were assumptions to check, not findings.

Four interviews

We wrote a semi-structured script — ice-breaker, the art scene, difficulties, cultural consumption, ways to bring artists and people closer — with separate questions for artists and for the public. Later we rewrote it to focus on illustrators, and added questions about starting out, being self-taught and contact with other artists.

We interviewed four artists, transcribed the recordings and cut them into quotes, tagged User 1 to 4. The quotes went into an affinity diagram, one row per theme: starting a career, distinct art markets, motivation to continue, art in everyday life, reaching an audience online, living from art, digital media, generations in art, pricing.

What came back was not mainly about sales. Several had started with few options and no one to point the way. Turning into “an artist” was hard against family expectations. There was no structured training on the business side of art, and beginners struggled to price their work. A large part of artists’ income came from other artists. Older generations found it harder to promote themselves on social media.

“I depend on myself… I’m the one who doesn’t have an agent.”

That line, from one of the illustrators, describes the person the product ended up being for: someone self-taught, running their own career, deciding alone when to stop drawing and start looking for work.

From pain to requirement

The scope plane turned the research into a list. Each row starts with a quote, the pain, then states what the user needs, then what the product must do. An artist with no agent, who has to plan their own growth, became a study track that guides the user. “From my generation on, we all have a hard time with media” became tools for managing social media posts, metrics and a calendar. Other rows asked for technical feedback on one’s work, places to work or exhibit, events and calls for artists, a way to find market opportunities without a middleman, chat between artists, and modules on negotiation.

What to build first

We placed every functional requirement on an impact × effort matrix. The study track, chat between artists and finding market opportunities sat in high impact and high effort. Technical feedback, a tab for events and calls, and a search for places to exhibit were high impact and lower effort. The social media tools landed in low impact and high effort, and the negotiation modules in low impact and low effort.

From there we drew six task flows, each a goal from start to end: take a lesson on the track, message another user, post a work for feedback, give feedback, sign up for an event, and apply to exhibit at one. The track flow sends a new user through a level check first, so the track can start where they are.

The flows set the site map: a home screen and five sections — Roadmap, with courses, modules and lessons; Explore, with projects to comment on; Events, down to taking part and exhibiting; Chat; and Profile.

Sketching the screens

The skeleton started with rough screens drawn digitally: home, explore, track, events. A second round was drawn by hand on paper, photographed and labelled on the board: splash, log in, sign up, a home feed for feedback, chat, creating a post, two versions of the track and two of events. Notes beside them ask for a screen to share location and a short quiz to place the user’s level. One note warns that the changes mean the flows must be updated.

We benchmarked by theme rather than by competitor: an events platform for the event screens, course platforms for the track, art-critique communities for feedback, portfolio networks for artists, and a mobile game for the way it shows progress. Three decisions came out of it. Home would be a feed of work for feedback. Events would become workshops. And the game’s progression would become levels on the track.

The navigation marked “Definitivo” at the end of the board has courses, community, progress, workshops and the user’s own area. Selling art, where the project started, isn’t in it.

The surface

The visual identity was designed by others on the team. Its brief: keep it simple, lean on the region, keep the feel of illustration and avoid looking too corporate. The mascot is the Maranguape frog, a species found only in Ceará, meant to work the way the owl works for Duolingo. The board holds seven drafts of it, with the team’s votes on sticky notes, and six palettes still to choose from.
