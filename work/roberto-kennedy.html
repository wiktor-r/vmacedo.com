How do you draw a photographer whose job is framing?

Roberto Kennedy photographs hotels, architecture and companies. He wanted a brand as serious as his clients. We gave him a monogram where two columns frame a single point of light — and no camera anywhere.

Roberto is a photographer with large clients — mostly hotels and companies. The briefing with him came down to four points: hotel and corporate work; seriousness; structures, because much of what he shoots is façades, columns and interiors; and framing, which for him is the essence of photography.

From those came three attributes for the brand — minimalism, precision and professionalism — and one constraint we set ourselves: the brand would have to live next to his photographs without competing with them.

Not a camera

Photographers' brands reach for the camera, the lens, the shutter. We sketched six directions — perspectives, flow, a spiral, calligraphy, a block, and a frame with a diamond at its centre — and kept the one that didn't depict equipment: an R and a mirrored K between two stems.

The stems do three jobs at once. They are the columns he photographs, they are the frame that crops a scene, and between them they hold the one thing a photographer actually captures: light.

Two stems delimit a point, the way a photographer delimits a scene.

Where the star comes from

The star in the gap is not decoration. Photograph a point of light with a small aperture and it opens into rays; that four-pointed spark is the shape in the monogram. In the presentation video, a photo darkens until only one point is left, the point opens into the spark, and the frame closes around it into the mark.

Built on one axis

The monogram is symmetric about a single axis. Both stems share the same thickness, a module m; the whole mark is twelve modules wide, and the star sits on the axis. Getting there took seven steps: the first sketch, the group's original R with a mirrored K, the K's arm crossing the R's bowl, symmetry, the top of the K released from the edge, adjustments to stems, serifs and the central gap, and finally the star.

A system, not a logo

The brief asked for versions, and each one had to be adjusted rather than derived. The primary sets the monogram over the name; the secondary runs horizontal for low, wide spaces like a website header. The simplified version is the monogram alone down to 24 pixels; below that, the frame symbol takes over and stays legible to 16. The line version drops the word Fotografia, and the negative thins the stems and name slightly so white on black doesn't swell.

There is no accent colour. The palette is six tones from black to white, because the brand's job is to step back from the photographs. Type is Cormorant for the logotype and titles, Jost for text.
