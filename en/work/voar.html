How can an NGO stay close to its individual donors?

Associação Voar, a non-profit in Fortaleza, receives regular gifts from individual donors. This case study follows a semester of design research on how the association relates to those donors, from a field visit and interviews to a tested app prototype and a published paper.

Associação Voar works with children, teenagers and their families in vulnerable situations in Fortaleza, offering education, culture, sport and leisure. Our group first visited its headquarters in September 2025, as part of the Design Research course.

The visit drew our attention to the individual donors, people who give to the association in their own name, and whose part in funding its activities was less visible. The national context made the question more pressing. According to the Doação Brasil survey, the share of Brazilians who donated money fell from 66% in 2015 to 52% in 2022, and the share who said they trusted NGOs fell from 59% to 47% over the same period.

The problem

The project dealt with Voar’s individual donors. They received little attention from the association, had little incentive to keep giving, and had a weak bond with it.

After the visit, each of us wrote down what we had seen and heard as short notes on a shared board. Among them: one room is used for meals, prayer and jiu-jitsu; there is a waiting list; the association follows 150 children in Fortaleza and 300 more in Tauá. We sorted the notes into needs, pains and opportunities.

Hypotheses and the CSD matrix

The project followed the Double Diamond, a design process in four phases: discover, define, develop and deliver. In the first two phases the group studied the problem before proposing any solution. We started from three hypotheses about donors: that the association lacked an effective way of showing them its impact, that it had no ongoing relationship with current or potential donors, and that it depended on one-off gifts, without a plan for recurring ones.

The field notes and a round of desk research on giving went into a CSD matrix — what we were certain of, what we supposed, what we doubted. Some of the certainties came from the reading: people give more when they see others giving, and the story of one identifiable person raises more than statistics about many. The doubts included the questions that guided the interviews: where does the money come from, are the current donation channels easy, and what is the first message a potential donor sees?

The doubts became a semi-structured interview script, and we ran three interviews with Voar staff to answer them: the coordinator, the person in charge of finance, and the executive director. We transcribed them and cut the answers into short quotes.

What the interviews said

The association’s income comes from companies, individual donors and public funding calls. Giving by individual donors is less predictable. In the words of the person in charge of finance: “they don’t follow a pattern — sometimes they give, sometimes they don’t, sometimes a bit more, sometimes a bit less.”

Most individual donors give by Pix. Others give through Doare, a third-party platform for recurring donations, which asks for a long sign-up and cancels the donation automatically when the donor’s card expires. A sponsorship campaign had used the platform for monthly gifts, and over time many of those gifts ended as donors’ cards expired or were replaced.

Many individual donors come from the church where Voar began, and they give because they know the place and the people. According to the coordinator, many of them still see the association as part of the church. Contact with donors was mostly personal: the executive director visited donors, above all the companies, while individual donors received little regular communication from the association.

“People don’t give to a number. They give to a person.”Coordinator of programmes and projects

The coordinator said this when asked what leads someone to give for the first time, pointing to the emotional bond formed at first contact and to the children’s stories. The idea carried into the app as one of its requirements: show donors the people their gifts support.

From pains to a product

In an affinity diagram session we grouped the quotes to find patterns, and set a goal for the product: to keep the donors Voar already has, bring them closer to the association, and show them what their gifts achieve. Three user requirements were chosen. The first is a simpler way in, so that a first donation does not begin with a long sign-up. The second is a donation channel that Voar controls; we looked at recurring Pix, so that an expired card does not end a donation. The third is continuous communication: stories of the children, and updates on where the money goes.

We aimed the app at smaller, recurring donors, the group with the least direct contact with the association. We benchmarked ShareTheMeal, the UN World Food Programme’s app, as the reference for a low-friction donation, looked again at the recurring-donation platform, whose sign-up form we marked as long, and drew four task flows: sign up and donate; follow the association’s activities and volunteer openings; buy from a small shop; and the donor’s own profile.

Testing it

We tested the low-fidelity prototype by having people use the application and carry out tasks in it. What we saw went into a mid-fidelity digital prototype — the version we delivered. The first digital version on our board has four tabs that follow the flows: Donate, once or monthly, ending in a Pix code; Community, with stories and volunteer openings; Shop, with campaigns and products; and Profile, with the monthly gift and a history of donations.

Notes left beside the screens on the board show what was still open. Let people type any amount, not only the preset ones. Add a place for feedback on donations. Let the history be viewed by date. And don’t put the total someone has given at the top of their profile — seeing how much you have spent isn’t always pleasant.

From project to paper

The project became an experience report, published in the proceedings of the VI Encontro de Curricularização da Extensão at Unifor in 2026.
